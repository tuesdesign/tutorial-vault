# Method #1: Virtual

### We've covered several examples of this. Computer programs can generate sound.

### In a DAW, the most common method is to use a VST instruments

### Fun fact! The VST SDK was recently made open source.
#### Thank you Steinberg, very cool!

---
# What can you do with virtual audio generation

### Virtual Synthesizers: Emulated, Granular, FM,  Wavetable, etc...
### Sample-Based Instruments and physical modeling
### "Romplers" (ie: soundfonts)

---
# Why use virtual audio generation
## (sometimes) Non-Destructive: Using MIDI, it is possible to change a composition, such as to fix errors.

## Predictable: Unlike with analogue audio, it is possible to achieve perfect control of a sound

## Simpler: Requires less equipment, is easier to set up, and generally has lower noise*

# Method #2: Analogue

### The common way to do this in a DAW is to arm a recording track attached to some form of ADC (analogue to digital converter) such as in an audio interface

### With this technique you can record input from musical instruments such as guitars, analogue synthesizers, as well as anything that can be picked up by a microphone (ie: vocals, piano, drums, etc)

---
# Why use analogue audio generation?

## Genuine: Virtual recreations of sounds and instruments are often flawed, a direct recording can give an end result which sounds more accurate   

## Emotive: MIDI signals cannot capture the same level of expression as a real instrument

## Imperfect: analogue signals will contain more 'flaws', which could be desirable

---

# Things to note...

## Destructive Recording: Unlike with digital audio, we cannot (easily) change the timing or pitch of notes after recording
#### It is possible using quantization and effects, but it is far from perfect or easy.

## Bigger Files: We must record and store all of the composite parts of our audio at the desired bit depth/sample rate. 

## Setup Complications: You will often need more equipment, a more controlled recording environment, and more patience.
